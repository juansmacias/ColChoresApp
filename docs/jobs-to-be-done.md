# Jobs to Be Done — Family Chores App

**Product:** Family Chores App
**Family profile:** 2 adults (Dad, Mom) + 2 children (ages approximate: 7–13)
**Framework:** Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) — Clayton Christensen / Bob Moesta

---

## Overview

When designing a family chores app, we focus on the underlying motivations that drive families to seek a solution — not just the features they ask for. Each job below is written in the canonical JTBD format:

> *When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].*

---

## Job 1 — Coordinate Without Conflict (The Orchestration Job)

**Primary user:** Mom and/or Dad (the household manager)

### Job Statement
When managing a household with two working parents and two kids, I want a single source of truth for who is responsible for each chore and when it is due, so I can stop being the one who has to remember, remind, and chase everyone down.

### Context
The household manager — often one parent more than the other — carries a disproportionate mental load. Nagging family members is emotionally exhausting and creates resentment. The "job" is not to create a chore list; it is to **offload the coordination overhead** from one person's brain to a shared system.

### Struggling moments (triggers)
- Sunday evening: trying to figure out who cleaned the bathroom last week
- Monday morning: everyone rushing out and nobody took out the trash
- End of week: one parent realizes they have been doing most of the chores silently
- Kids fighting over who "did it last time"

### Success criteria
- No parent needs to verbally remind anyone more than once per week
- Every family member knows their responsibilities without asking
- Disputes about fairness can be resolved by checking the app, not by memory

---

## Job 2 — Build Responsibility in Children (The Parenting Job)

**Primary user:** Mom and/or Dad (the educator), with kids as secondary participants

### Job Statement
When I want my children to grow into responsible, self-sufficient adults, I want a structured and motivating way to teach them that contributing to the household is a normal part of being in a family, so I can gradually give them more autonomy without the household falling apart.

### Context
Parents are not just trying to get chores done — they are using chores as a **parenting tool**. The app must support teaching, not just task management. Kids need age-appropriate tasks, clear instructions, and some form of feedback loop (reward, acknowledgment, or progress) that makes contribution feel meaningful rather than punitive.

### Struggling moments (triggers)
- A parent assigns a chore that the child says "they don't know how to do"
- A child refuses because the task feels unfair compared to a sibling's
- A parent wants to increase responsibility as the child gets older but has no structured way to do it
- Allowance conversations: a parent wants to tie chores to rewards but has no tracking mechanism

### Success criteria
- Kids can check their own chores without asking a parent
- There is a visible progress or reward mechanism the child finds motivating
- Parents can adjust task difficulty as the child's age and ability increase
- Both children feel their workload is equitable

---

## Job 3 — Make the Invisible Work Visible (The Fairness Job)

**Primary user:** Both parents (and older kids)

### Job Statement
When household labor feels unevenly distributed, I want a way to see who is actually contributing and how much, so I can have productive, fact-based conversations about fairness instead of emotionally charged arguments.

### Context
Unequal chore distribution is one of the most common sources of friction in households. The problem is rarely malicious — it is that invisible work (cleaning, mental load, planning) is simply not tracked. Both parents may believe they are doing more than their partner. Older kids may feel they do more than a younger sibling. The "job" is not to police effort; it is to create **shared visibility that enables honest conversation**.

### Struggling moments (triggers)
- One parent says "I feel like I do everything around here" — and there is no data to agree or disagree with
- A parent returns from a business trip and cannot see what happened at home
- A child complains a sibling has easier chores
- End-of-week or end-of-month review: the family wants to celebrate contributions or address gaps

### Success criteria
- Any family member can see a summary of who completed what, over any recent period
- The data is neutral and non-judgmental — it informs discussion, not blame
- One parent can check household status remotely (traveling, at work)
- The family can use the data to adjust assignments proactively, before frustration builds

## Job Priority Summary

| # | Job | Core Anxiety Resolved | Primary Beneficiary |
|---|-----|-----------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Coordinate without conflict | Mental load / nagging | Parent (household manager) |
| 2 | Build responsibility in children | Parenting effectiveness | Parent + Kids |
| 3 | Make the invisible work visible | Fairness and resentment | Both parents + older kids |

---

## Notes for Product Design

- **Job 1** drives initial adoption — families adopt the app because the current system (verbal reminders, whiteboards, sticky notes) is breaking down.
- **Job 2** drives long-term retention — parents keep using it because it is working as a parenting strategy.
- **Job 3** drives family-wide engagement — if only one parent uses it, the fairness job cannot be satisfied.
- All three jobs require **low friction for kids** — if the child interface is cumbersome, the product fails jobs 1 and 2 simultaneously.
